# Calendar Planning

A calendar is more than a list of meetings. Used well, it shows what needs attention, protects focused work, and helps people coordinate without constant back-and-forth.

This guide covers practical calendar planning for individual work, shared schedules, deadlines, and recurring commitments.

## When to use this guide

Use this guide when you need to:

- Plan a week of work.
- Schedule meetings with several people.
- Protect time for focused work.
- Track deadlines and reminders.
- Avoid overlapping commitments.
- Make recurring work easier to manage.

## Start with fixed commitments

Begin by adding anything that cannot easily move.

Examples include:

- Meetings that are already confirmed.
- Customer or partner calls.
- Deadlines.
- Travel time.
- Leave or personal appointments.
- Time-sensitive tasks.

Once fixed commitments are visible, it is easier to decide where flexible work belongs.

## Block focused work

Important work often needs uninterrupted time. Put focus blocks on the calendar before the week fills up.

Good focus blocks are:

- Specific enough to be useful.
- Long enough to complete meaningful work.
- Placed at times when you normally have energy.
- Protected from avoidable meetings.

Examples:

- Draft monthly report
- Review budget spreadsheet
- Prepare client presentation
- Process support queue

Avoid vague blocks such as Work time or Catch up unless they are only placeholders.

## Use calendar titles that explain the purpose

A clear event title helps everyone understand why time is reserved.

Prefer:

- Review sales forecast
- Approve final invoice list
- Plan onboarding checklist
- Prepare Friday project update

Avoid:

- Meeting
- Sync
- Discussion
- Admin

If the title is vague, add a short description or agenda.

## Add useful event details

For meetings, include enough detail that people can arrive prepared.

Useful details include:

- Purpose of the meeting.
- Desired outcome.
- Agenda or talking points.
- Links to documents.
- Call or location details.
- Preparation needed.

A meeting without a purpose is harder to run and easier to skip.

## Choose the right duration

Not every meeting needs 30 or 60 minutes.

Use shorter blocks when the work is simple:

- 10 minutes for a quick decision.
- 15 minutes for a check-in.
- 25 minutes for a focused discussion.
- 45 minutes for review and decisions.

Leave longer blocks for planning, workshops, interviews, or complex reviews.

## Leave buffer time

Back-to-back commitments create delays and rushed decisions.

Add buffer time when:

- Meetings involve different topics.
- You need to prepare before a call.
- You need to write notes afterward.
- Travel or setup time is required.
- The previous meeting may run long.

Even a five-minute gap can reduce mistakes and missed follow-ups.

## Plan recurring work carefully

Recurring calendar events are useful when the work truly repeats.

Good recurring events include:

- Weekly planning.
- Monthly reporting.
- Regular review meetings.
- Routine maintenance tasks.
- Reminder blocks for known deadlines.

Review recurring events often. Remove or shorten events that no longer have a clear purpose.

## Use reminders deliberately

Reminders should help you act at the right time. Too many reminders become background noise.

Use reminders for:

- Deadlines.
- Preparation time.
- Time-sensitive follow-ups.
- Meetings that require materials.
- Tasks that are easy to forget.

Set the reminder early enough to do something useful, not just to notice the event.

## Coordinate across time zones

When working with people in different places, check the local time for everyone involved.

Before sending an invitation:

- Confirm the meeting time is reasonable for each participant.
- Include the time zone if the audience may need it.
- Avoid assuming everyone works the same hours.
- Be careful with daylight saving changes.

For important meetings, include the date and time in the message as well as the calendar invite.

## Keep private information out of shared calendars

Calendar entries may be visible to more people than expected.

Avoid putting sensitive information in event titles, descriptions, locations, or attachments.

Use neutral titles when needed, and share private detail through the appropriate secure channel.

## Review the week ahead

A short weekly calendar review helps prevent overload.

Check:

- Are the most important tasks scheduled?
- Are there conflicting meetings?
- Are deadlines visible?
- Is there time to prepare for key meetings?
- Are there too many meetings in one day?
- Can any meeting be shortened, moved, or canceled?

Move work intentionally instead of letting the calendar fill by default.

## Review the day ahead

At the start or end of each day, scan the next day.

Look for:

- First meeting time.
- Preparation needed.
- Files or links to gather.
- Follow-ups from previous meetings.
- Gaps that can be used for focused work.
- Meetings that need an agenda or decision.

A two-minute review can prevent a rushed morning.

## When to decline or reschedule

It is reasonable to decline or reschedule when:

- The purpose is unclear.
- The meeting conflicts with higher-priority work.
- The right people are not included.
- The decision can be handled by message.
- You need more time to prepare.

When declining, be brief and helpful. Suggest a better time, ask for the desired outcome, or offer an alternative way to resolve the issue.

## Quick checklist

Before the week starts:

- Fixed commitments are added.
- Deadlines are visible.
- Focus blocks are protected.
- Meeting titles are clear.
- Important meetings have agendas.
- Recurring events still make sense.
- Time zones are checked where needed.
- Sensitive details are kept out of shared calendar fields.

## Key takeaway

A good calendar shows priorities, not just availability. Put important work on the calendar, make meetings clear, and leave enough room to prepare and follow through.
