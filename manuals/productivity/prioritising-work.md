# Prioritising Work

Use this guide when deciding which tasks, requests, or follow-up items should be handled first.

Good prioritisation helps people spend time on the work that matters most, reduce avoidable delays, and make trade-offs visible.

## Start With The Outcome

Before ranking work, confirm what each item is meant to achieve.

Ask:

- What problem does this solve?
- Who benefits from it?
- What decision or deadline does it support?
- What happens if it is delayed?
- What does complete look like?

If the outcome is unclear, the item may need clarification before it can be prioritised.

## Separate Urgent From Important

Urgent work needs attention soon. Important work has meaningful value or risk.

Some work is both urgent and important. Some work feels urgent because it is visible, noisy, or recently requested.

Before moving something to the top of the list, check whether it is genuinely time-sensitive or simply new.

## Consider Impact

Impact describes the value or consequence of completing the work.

High-impact work may:

- Help a customer or stakeholder.
- Prevent a problem.
- Support a deadline.
- Improve quality.
- Reduce repeated manual effort.
- Enable other work to move forward.

Low-impact work may still be worth doing, but it should not always displace work with broader value.

## Consider Effort

Effort describes the time, complexity, or coordination needed.

When estimating effort, consider:

- How much work is required.
- Whether the task needs input from others.
- Whether information is missing.
- Whether review or approval is needed.
- Whether the task has hidden complexity.

Small tasks with clear value can often be completed quickly. Large tasks may need to be split before prioritising.

## Consider Risk

Risk helps identify work that may cause problems if ignored.

Risk may include:

- Customer impact.
- Financial impact.
- Legal or compliance impact.
- Operational disruption.
- Data quality issues.
- Missed deadlines.
- Repeated manual work.

If a task has high risk, make sure the risk is visible even if the work cannot start immediately.

## Check Dependencies

Some tasks unlock other work.

A task may be higher priority if:

- Other tasks depend on it.
- Someone is waiting for the result.
- It provides information needed for a decision.
- It removes a blocker.
- It must be done before review or delivery.

Track dependencies clearly so blocked work does not appear to be ignored.

## Use Priority Labels Carefully

Priority labels are useful only when everyone understands them.

A simple priority set may include:

- High: needs attention soon because impact, risk, or dependency is significant.
- Medium: valuable but not immediately blocking.
- Low: useful but can wait.
- Waiting: cannot move forward until something else happens.

Avoid marking too many items as high priority. If everything is high priority, the label stops helping.

## Make Trade-Offs Visible

Prioritising one task often means delaying another.

When priorities change, note:

- What moved up.
- What moved down.
- Why the change was made.
- Who needs to know.
- Whether any deadline or expectation changed.

Clear trade-offs help avoid confusion and repeated follow-up.

## Review Priorities Regularly

Priorities can change as new information arrives.

During review, check:

- New urgent items.
- Stale high-priority items.
- Blocked work.
- Tasks with approaching deadlines.
- Tasks that no longer matter.
- Work that should be split or clarified.

A short regular review keeps the list accurate.

## When To Ask For Help

Ask for help prioritising when:

- Several tasks have the same deadline.
- A task affects customers, money, legal obligations, or operations.
- The impact is unclear.
- The owner cannot decide what to delay.
- A blocker needs a decision.
- The task list is too large to manage confidently.

It is better to ask early than to silently choose the wrong trade-off.

## Quick Checklist

Before finalising priorities:

- Each item has a clear outcome.
- Urgent and important work have been separated.
- Impact, effort, risk, and dependencies are considered.
- High-priority labels are used sparingly.
- Blocked items are visible.
- Trade-offs are recorded.
- Priorities have a review date or review habit.
- Unclear items are flagged for clarification.
