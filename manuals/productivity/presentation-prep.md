# Presentation Prep

A good presentation helps people understand a topic and decide what to do next. It does not need to include everything you know. It needs to make the main point clear.

This guide covers practical preparation for work presentations, project updates, proposals, training sessions, and review meetings.

## When to use this guide

Use this guide when you need to:

- Prepare a slide deck.
- Give a project update.
- Explain a recommendation.
- Present options for a decision.
- Train a group on a process.
- Share findings from a review or analysis.

## Start with the audience

Before making slides, decide who the presentation is for.

Ask:

- What does the audience already know?
- What do they need to understand?
- What decision or action do they need to take?
- How much time do they have?
- What questions are they likely to ask?

A presentation for decision-makers should be shorter and more outcome-focused than a presentation for people doing the work.

## Define the main message

Write the main message in one sentence before building the deck.

Examples:

- The project is on track, but the timeline depends on approval by Friday.
- Option two is the best choice because it reduces risk and can be delivered sooner.
- The report shows higher demand in two regions and lower demand in one region.

If the main message is unclear, the slides will usually become unclear too.

## Choose the purpose of each slide

Every slide should have a job.

Common slide purposes include:

- Introduce the topic.
- Summarize the recommendation.
- Show progress.
- Compare options.
- Explain a risk.
- Support a decision.
- List next steps.

If a slide does not help the audience understand or act, remove it or move it to an appendix.

## Use a simple structure

A clear presentation usually follows this order:

1. Purpose.
2. Summary.
3. Context.
4. Main points.
5. Recommendation or decision.
6. Next steps.

For short updates, this can be only a few slides. For longer presentations, keep the same order but add detail where needed.

## Put the conclusion early

Do not make the audience wait until the end to understand the point.

Use an early summary slide that says:

- What changed.
- What matters.
- What you recommend.
- What decision or action is needed.

Details can follow after the conclusion.

## Keep slides readable

Slides should support what you say. They should not be full documents.

Use:

- Short headings.
- Simple bullets.
- Clear labels.
- Enough spacing.
- One main idea per slide.

Avoid:

- Dense paragraphs.
- Tiny text.
- Too many charts on one slide.
- Unexplained abbreviations.
- Decorative elements that distract from the content.

## Make charts and tables easy to understand

Charts and tables should answer a clear question.

Before using one, decide:

- What should the audience notice?
- Which number matters most?
- Is comparison needed?
- Is the chart easier than a sentence?

Use labels and titles that explain the point. If the chart needs a long explanation, simplify it or split it into more than one slide.

## Prepare for questions

List likely questions before presenting.

Questions often cover:

- Cost.
- Timing.
- Risk.
- Alternatives.
- Source data.
- Ownership.
- Impact on customers or teams.
- What happens if no decision is made.

Prepare short answers and keep supporting details ready. Do not overload the main slides just to answer every possible question.

## Check the flow

Review the deck from the audience's point of view.

Check:

- Does the first slide explain why the presentation matters?
- Does each slide lead naturally to the next?
- Is the recommendation clear?
- Are there any repeated points?
- Are important details missing?
- Is the deck too long for the time available?

If the flow feels difficult, simplify the order before improving visual polish.

## Practice the opening and close

The opening should set context quickly.

A useful opening covers:

- What the presentation is about.
- Why it matters.
- What outcome is needed.

The close should make the next step clear.

A useful close covers:

- The recommendation.
- The decision or action needed.
- The owner.
- The timing.

## Prepare the file

Before sending or presenting, confirm that:

- The file name is clear.
- The latest version is being used.
- Links work.
- Fonts and images display correctly.
- Notes or private comments are removed if the file will be shared.
- Any appendix material is intentional.

If the presentation includes private or sensitive information, confirm the audience and sharing method before sending it.

## Present calmly

During the presentation:

- Start with the purpose.
- Move at a steady pace.
- Explain the main point of each slide.
- Pause for important questions.
- Keep the discussion tied to the needed outcome.
- Note follow-ups when an answer is not available.

It is better to say that you will follow up than to guess.

## After the presentation

Send a short follow-up when useful.

Include:

- Decision made.
- Open questions.
- Action items.
- Owners.
- Due dates.
- Link or attachment for the final deck.

The follow-up should be shorter than the presentation.

## Quick checklist

Before presenting, confirm that:

- The audience is clear.
- The main message is one sentence.
- The conclusion appears early.
- Each slide has a purpose.
- Charts and tables are labeled.
- Questions have been anticipated.
- The file is the current version.
- Private notes or comments are removed.
- The closing next step is clear.

## Key takeaway

A strong presentation is built around one clear message. Know the audience, show the conclusion early, keep slides readable, and end with a specific next step.
