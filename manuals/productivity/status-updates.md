# Status Updates

A good status update helps people understand what changed, what is on track, what is at risk, and what decision or help is needed.

This guide covers practical status updates for projects, tasks, documents, spreadsheets, customer work, and recurring operations.

## When to use this guide

Use this guide when you need to:

- Share progress on a task or project.
- Explain a delay or blocker.
- Prepare a weekly update.
- Summarize work after a meeting.
- Ask for a decision.
- Tell people what will happen next.

## Start with the headline

Open with the most important point.

Examples:

- The report is on track for Friday.
- The file is ready for review, but the totals need confirmation.
- The task is blocked until the source data is corrected.
- The first draft is complete and needs approval by Thursday.

Do not make readers search for the status. Put it in the first sentence or first bullet.

## Use a consistent structure

A simple structure makes updates easier to scan.

Recommended sections:

- Status
- Completed
- In progress
- Blocked
- Risks
- Next steps
- Help needed

For short updates, use only the sections that matter.

## Be specific about progress

Specific updates are more useful than general statements.

Prefer:

- The Summary tab is complete, and the Detail tab needs one more review.
- Three of five sections are drafted.
- The file was sent for review on Tuesday.
- The customer response is still pending.

Avoid:

- Making progress
- Almost done
- Looking good
- Working on it

If you use a broad phrase, add detail that explains what it means.

## Separate completed work from planned work

Readers should be able to tell what has happened and what is still expected.

Completed:

- Drafted the report introduction.
- Updated the spreadsheet totals.
- Sent the review request.

Next:

- Confirm final numbers.
- Apply reviewer changes.
- Send the final file by Friday.

This prevents planned work from sounding finished.

## Call out blockers clearly

If progress is blocked, explain the blocker directly.

Include:

- What is blocked.
- Why it is blocked.
- Who or what is needed.
- What has already been tried.
- When the next follow-up will happen.

Example:

The final file is blocked because two figures are missing from the source spreadsheet. I sent a follow-up this morning and will check again tomorrow at 10 AM.

## Name risks early

A risk is something that might cause delay, rework, or a poor outcome.

Useful risk updates include:

- What might happen.
- Why it matters.
- How likely it is.
- What can reduce the risk.
- When a decision is needed.

Example:

There is a risk that the review will miss Friday if approval is not received by Thursday afternoon. The simplest mitigation is to approve the current draft or identify required changes today.

## Make requests actionable

If you need help, make the request specific.

Prefer:

- Please confirm the totals in the Summary tab by 3 PM Thursday.
- Please choose option one or option two before Friday's meeting.
- Please send the missing file name so I can attach the final version.

Avoid:

- Thoughts?
- Please advise
- Can someone look at this?

A clear request should name the action, owner, and timing.

## Use status labels carefully

Status labels can help, but only if they are defined.

Common labels:

- On track: work is expected to finish on time.
- At risk: work may miss the expected time or quality target.
- Blocked: work cannot continue until something changes.
- Complete: no further work is needed except optional review or archive.

Do not mark work complete if approval, delivery, or a required check is still pending.

## Keep updates short

Most status updates should be short enough to read quickly.

Include only:

- Important progress.
- Meaningful changes.
- Risks or blockers.
- Decisions needed.
- Next steps.

Move background detail into a linked document or appendix when needed.

## Match the update to the audience

Different readers need different levels of detail.

For leadership or decision-makers, focus on:

- Outcome.
- Timeline.
- Risk.
- Decision needed.

For people doing the work, include:

- Specific tasks.
- File locations.
- Dependencies.
- Owners.
- Detailed next steps.

For customers or partners, keep the update clear, factual, and free of internal-only detail.

## Update before people ask

A timely update reduces follow-up messages.

Send an update when:

- A deadline changes.
- A blocker appears.
- A decision is needed.
- A milestone is reached.
- Work is complete.
- Nothing has changed, but people are waiting.

A short no-change update is better than silence when people are relying on the work.

## Protect private information

Status updates are often forwarded or copied into other places.

Avoid including:

- Passwords or access tokens.
- Private customer data unless required and approved.
- Internal notes that should not be shared.
- Unrelated message history.
- Sensitive file links for audiences that should not access them.

If sensitive detail is required, share it through the appropriate secure channel.

## Status update template

Use this template for a clear update.

Status:
[On track, at risk, blocked, or complete]

Headline:
[The most important point]

Completed:
- [Completed item]
- [Completed item]

In progress:
- [Current work]

Blocked or at risk:
- [Blocker or risk, or write None]

Next steps:
- [Next action]
- [Owner and timing]

Help needed:
- [Specific request, or write None]

## Quick checklist

Before sending a status update, confirm that:

- The headline is clear.
- Completed work is separate from planned work.
- Blockers are named.
- Risks are visible early.
- Requests are specific.
- Owners and dates are included when needed.
- The update is appropriate for the audience.
- Private information is not exposed.

## Key takeaway

A strong status update makes the current state obvious. Say what changed, what is next, what is blocked, and what help or decision is needed.
